From Voltage to Decision

Estimated time: ~1.5 hours  |  Sections: 2.1–2.2  |  Prerequisite: Week 2 Orientation

What We Have / What We Can't Do / What We Can Now Do

What you already know: Data is stored as patterns of 0s and 1s, where 0 means no voltage and 1 means voltage present.

What you can't do yet: Explain what sits between "voltage on a wire" and "the machine decided something." Right now that is a black box.

What you will be able to do by the end of this page: Describe the transistor as a voltage-controlled switch, read and write truth tables for all seven logic gates, and recognize their circuit symbols on sight.

Last week you learned that any data can be represented as 0s and 1s. You converted between binary, hexadecimal, and decimal. You saw that the same byte pattern can mean an integer, a character, or a color — depending on how the CPU interprets it.

But representation is passive. The bits just sit there.

How does the computer actually do anything with those bits? How does it add two numbers, compare values, or decide whether to execute one instruction or another? How does voltage — which is either present or absent — become a decision?

The answer is the logic gate. A logic gate is a tiny circuit that takes one or more binary inputs and produces a binary output according to a fixed rule. These rules seem trivial, but they are the atoms of computation. Every operation your computer performs — from rendering this page to running a compiler — is the result of billions of logic gates making billions of tiny decisions per second.

2.1 — From Voltage to Decision: Transistors as Switches

Last week we said that 0 means "no voltage" and 1 means "voltage present." But how does voltage become a decision? The bridge is the transistor.

A transistor is a tiny electronic switch. In its simplest role in digital logic, it has three terminals:

  • Gate — the control input. Voltage here determines whether the switch is open or closed.
  • Source — where current enters.
  • Drain — where current exits.
A transistor used as a switch. Voltage on the gate decides whether current may pass from source to drain.

When voltage is applied to the gate, the transistor conducts current from source to drain — the switch is ON, and the output is 1. When no voltage is applied, the transistor blocks current — the switch is OFF, and the output is 0.

This is the entire physical mechanism. A transistor converts a voltage signal into a decision: let current through, or don't. Modern CPUs contain billions of transistors, each making this binary decision billions of times per second.

We do not need to understand semiconductor physics to work with digital logic. We need exactly three things:

  1. A transistor is a voltage-controlled switch.
  2. Transistors can be wired together to form logic gates.
  3. Logic gates are the building blocks of all digital computation.

That is the last we will say about physics. From here on, the transistor is a switch and we build upward.

2.2 — The Basic Gates: AND, OR, NOT

Logic gates are the vocabulary of digital computation. Every complex circuit — from a simple adder to a complete CPU — is built from combinations of a small set of basic gates. We start with the three fundamental ones.

The AND Gate

The AND gate outputs 1 if and only if all of its inputs are 1. Think of it as a circuit that requires every condition to be met.

ABA AND B
000
010
100
111

In Boolean algebra, A AND B is written A · B or simply AB.

You already know this from programming

When you write a compound conditional in code — if (loggedIn && hasPermission) — you are describing an AND gate. The body runs only when both conditions are true. An AND gate is exactly that, in hardware: two wires in, one wire out, output high only when both inputs are high.

The || operator is an OR gate. The ! operator is a NOT gate. You have been programming with logic gates the whole time; you just were not holding one.

The OR Gate

The OR gate outputs 1 if at least one of its inputs is 1.

ABA OR B
000
011
101
111

In Boolean algebra, A OR B is written A + B. Note that this + does not mean addition — 1 + 1 = 1 here, not 2. It is the OR operator, and the resemblance to arithmetic is a historical accident you will get used to.

The NOT Gate (Inverter)

The NOT gate has one input and one output. It flips the input.

ANOT A
01
10

In Boolean algebra, NOT A is written A' or ¬A. This course uses the prime notation A' almost everywhere.

Reading Circuit Symbols

Each gate has a standard symbol you will see in Logisim, in textbooks, and on datasheets for the rest of your career:

  • AND — flat input side, rounded output side (a capital D).
  • OR — curved input side, pointed output side.
  • NOT — a triangle with a small circle at the output.

That small circle is the important part. In circuit notation, a circle at an output means "inverted." Once you know that, you can read gates you have never seen: an AND symbol with a circle is a NAND (NOT-AND), and an OR symbol with a circle is a NOR (NOT-OR). You will meet both on the next page, and the circle is how you will recognize them.

Why start with these three? AND, OR, and NOT are the most intuitive gates for humans, because they map directly onto everyday logic and onto code you have already written. But they are not the most efficient to manufacture. That turns out to matter enormously, and it is where the next page begins.

What Comes Next

Forward reference

AND, OR, and NOT are the gates that match how humans think. They are not the gates that chip factories preferred to build. On the next page you will meet a single gate that can be wired together to produce all three of these — and therefore any circuit whatsoever, including a complete CPU.

That gate is the NAND gate, and the fact that one gate type is sufficient for all of computing is the most important idea in this week and arguably in this course.
