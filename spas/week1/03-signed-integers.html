Signed Integers & Two's Complement

Estimated time: ~2.0 hours  |  Sections: 1.6–1.7  |  Prerequisite: SPA 2 (Converting Between Bases)

What We Have / What We Can't Do / What We Can Now Do

What you already know: You can convert fluently between decimal, binary, hex, and octal. You understand unsigned integer representation and the concept of overflow from SPA 2.

What you can't do yet: Represent negative numbers in binary. The unsigned system only handles values from 0 upward. But every real program needs negative numbers. How do you encode −5 in a fixed number of bits?

What you will be able to do by the end of this SPA: Represent any signed integer in three different formats (sign-magnitude, one's complement, two's complement). Understand why two's complement is the universal choice used by every modern CPU. Perform the flip-and-add-1 derivation for any negative value.

In SPA 2 you learned that n unsigned bits can represent values from 0 to 2n−1. But programs need negative numbers too. How do we encode a sign in a fixed number of bits?

Three historical approaches existed. Only one survived. Understanding all three — and why the others failed — is critical to understanding how your CPU actually works.

1.6 — Signed Integers: Three Representations

Sign-Magnitude

Use the leftmost (most significant) bit as a sign: 0 = positive, 1 = negative. The remaining bits are the magnitude.

+6 in 8-bit sign-magnitude:  00000110
-6 in 8-bit sign-magnitude:  10000110

Problem: Two representations of zero: 00000000 (+0) and 10000000 (−0). Hardware must special-case both. Arithmetic circuits become more complex because the sign must be handled separately from the magnitude.

One's Complement

Negative numbers are formed by flipping every bit of the positive value.

+6 in 8-bit:      00000110
-6 in 1's comp:   11111001  (flip every bit)

Problem: Still two zeros: 00000000 (+0) and 11111111 (−0). Also requires "end-around carry" in addition, which complicates the adder circuit.

Two's Complement

Negative numbers are formed by flipping every bit and adding 1.

+6 in 8-bit:      00000110
Flip all bits:    11111001
Add 1:            11111010  = -6 in two's complement

Why it works: In two's complement, the leftmost bit has a negative place value. For an 8-bit value:

Position:  7      6   5   4   3   2   1   0
Value:   -128    64  32  16  8   4   2   1
Bits:     1      1   1   1  0   1   0   0

Value = -128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 4 = -12

Range of 8-bit two's complement: −128 to +127 (not symmetric — one more negative value because zero takes the +0 slot).

Key Insight

The range is asymmetric: −128 to +127. There is no +128 in 8-bit two's complement. The bit pattern 10000000 represents −128 (not −0). This is because −128 = −128 + 0, and there is no room for a separate −0 representation.

1.7 — Why Two's Complement Wins

Two's complement is not just a convention. It is the representation that makes hardware simplest. Here is why:

1. Single representation of zero

There is only one zero: 00000000. No special-casing needed in comparisons or arithmetic.

2. Addition works the same for all sign combinations

You can add two positive numbers, two negative numbers, or a positive and a negative number using the exact same adder circuit. The hardware does not need to check the signs first.

Example: 5 + (−3) in 4-bit two's complement:

  5 = 0101
- 3 = 1101   (flip 0011 → 1100, add 1 → 1101)
-------
       0010  = 2  (carry out of bit 3 is discarded)

The same adder circuit that computes 5 + 3 also computes 5 + (−3). This is not a coincidence — it is a mathematical property of two's complement that makes the ALU (built Week 6) dramatically simpler.

3. Subtraction is just addition

To compute A − B, negate B (two's complement) and add. No separate subtraction circuit needed.

4. The most significant bit is a natural sign indicator

If the MSB is 0, the value is non-negative. If 1, it is negative. No extra wire or flag needed.

Universal Standard

Every modern CPU uses two's complement for signed integers. MIPS, x86, ARM — all of them. When you write -5 in C or Java, the compiler stores it as the two's complement bit pattern. When you write assembly in Week 6, immediate values in instructions are encoded in two's complement.
